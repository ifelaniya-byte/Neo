A model can be excellent at one-step prediction yet fail during planning because small errors compound over imagined trajectories. Ensemble-based epistemic uncertainty is useful specifically because it helps detect those untrustworthy rollouts.

For your DeFi-style environment, the meaningful “world state” is not generic physics. It is a time-indexed market-and-execution state: reserves/liquidity, pool fee tiers, pending-state proxies, gas conditions, slippage, quote staleness, routing, execution latency, block timing, and realized PnL net of all costs.

The 16-part architecture
Use these as integrated subsystems, not independently marketed “power multipliers.”

#	Subsystem	Purpose	DeFi / execution interpretation
1	Schema and feature contract	Prevent input drift and leakage	Canonical state snapshot with block number, timestamp, sources, freshness
2	Multi-resolution encoder	Separate tick, block, minute, and regime dynamics	Short-term mempool/gas versus slower liquidity and volatility changes
3	Temporal latent model	Compress history into a predictive state	Learns latent market and execution regime
4	Structured graph encoder	Represent changing relationships	Tokens, pools, venues, bridges, routers, and liquidity edges
5	Action-conditioned transition model	Predict next state after an action	Simulate route, size, gas bid, inclusion strategy, and unwind
6	Probabilistic dynamics head	Predict distributions, not point estimates	Slippage, confirmation time, reverts, PnL ranges
7	Bootstrap ensemble	Estimate epistemic/model uncertainty	Detect unobserved pools, regimes, and sparse-data conditions
8	Conformal calibration layer	Make confidence operationally meaningful	“80% confidence” should approximate 80% empirical coverage
9	Regime/change-point detector	Identify nonstationarity	Liquidity migration, volatility shock, MEV competition change
10	Causal hypothesis module	Test interventions rather than assume causality	Estimate effect of your trade size or gas bid on execution outcome
11	Counterfactual simulator	Compare action alternatives fairly	Same observed state, different route/size/submit policy
12	Retrieval memory	Reuse similar historical regimes	Pull prior periods with similar liquidity, gas, and volatility
13	Constraint and invariant layer	Reject impossible or unsafe predictions	Nonnegative balances, pool math, capital limits, gas accounting
14	Risk-sensitive MPC planner	Optimize action sequences under uncertainty	Maximize lower-confidence-bound net PnL rather than mean PnL
15	Abstention and fallback policy	Do nothing when confidence is insufficient	Trade only when predicted edge exceeds uncertainty and cost buffers
16	Evaluation and continual-learning loop	Prevent self-deception and drift	Walk-forward backtests, paper trading, delayed labels, rollbackable retraining
Causal components should be treated as hypothesis-driven modules, not automatic truth engines: causal representation learning faces identification problems under hidden confounding and often needs strong assumptions or intervention-quality data.

16 CAPABILITIES, IMPLEMENTED COMPACTLY
Do not create sixteen unrelated neural networks. Use a shared latent state with
small task heads and explicit ablations.

1. Canonical time-causal data contract:
   Pydantic models; validation of timestamps, block ordering, staleness,
   missingness, schema compatibility, and feature leakage.

2. Multi-resolution temporal features:
   Use rolling windows for short/medium/slow frequencies, encoded with a
   lightweight temporal transformer or GRU. Make model choice configurable.

3. Dynamic protocol graph:
   Nodes = tokens/pools/venues/routers; edges = swaps/liquidity/bridge links.
   Begin with graph summary features; add a small GAT/GraphSAGE encoder only if
   its ablation improves held-out net metrics.

4. Shared latent encoder:
   Fuse tabular market features, execution features, temporal history, action
   encoding, and optional graph embedding into z_t.

5. Action-conditioned world model:
   Predict next state deltas and outcome distribution conditioned on z_t and
   candidate action a_t.

6. Multi-task heads:
   Predict:
   - net PnL distribution
   - gas cost distribution
   - slippage distribution
   - revert probability
   - inclusion-before-deadline probability
   - next-state deltas
   Use weighted losses defined in config.

7. Bootstrap ensemble:
   Train 5 compact independent members with distinct seeds/bootstrap samples.
   Mean estimates are predictions; disagreement estimates epistemic uncertainty.

8. Aleatoric uncertainty:
   Use heteroscedastic Gaussian or quantile heads for outcome noise. Do not
   confuse aleatoric variance with ensemble disagreement.

9. Calibration:
   Fit split-conformal residual calibrators per major regime and output interval.
   Recalibrate on a rolling recent window. Log empirical coverage. Calibration
   is valid only under its assumptions; monitor drift and abstain under major
   shift rather than claiming permanent guarantees.

10. OOD and data-quality detection:
    Combine latent Mahalanobis distance, ensemble disagreement, feature
    missingness, input staleness, and regime-change score into an OOD score.
    Thresholds come from validation quantiles, never hand-waved constants.

11. Regime detection:
    Implement simple, robust change-point/regime features first:
    rolling volatility, liquidity movement, gas movement, spread behavior, and
    CUSUM or Bayesian online change-point approximation. Regime labels feed
    calibrators and evaluation slices.

12. Retrieval memory:
    Store historical latent vectors, action descriptors, and outcomes in local
    FAISS or sklearn nearest-neighbor index. Retrieve only past observations.
    Add retrieved aggregate outcomes to the planner context.

13. Structural constraints:
    Constraint engine checks nonnegative balances, capital availability, known
    pool/route limits, slippage limit, gas limit, quote staleness, deadline,
    allowlist, and economic invariant checks. Failure always returns ABSTAIN.

14. Conservative planner:
    For each candidate action, optionally perturb continuous size over a bounded
    grid or CEM. Score:
      score(a) = mean_net_pnl
                 - lambda_u * total_uncertainty
                 - lambda_c * CVaR_alpha(loss)
                 - expected_gas
                 - expected_slippage
                 - penalty(revert/inclusion risk)
    Support direct action scoring first. Do NOT implement expensive long-horizon
    MPC until one-step and short-horizon prediction/calibration prove useful.

15. Abstention gate:
    TRADE iff all:
      lower_confidence_bound_net_pnl > min_net_edge_usd
      revert_probability <= max_revert_probability
      inclusion_probability >= min_inclusion_probability
      ood_score <= calibrated_ood_threshold
      all hard constraints pass
      data freshness passes
    Otherwise output ABSTAIN with explicit reason codes.

16. Walk-forward evaluation and continual learning:
    Time-ordered train/validation/calibration/test windows. Evaluate per regime,
    per liquidity bucket, and per action type. Support retraining only through
    a versioned offline job; no uncontrolled online weight updates in production.

Do not add 16 disconnected neural components. The density comes from one shared latent representation feeding multiple heads, an ensemble for epistemic uncertainty, calibrated intervals, and a constraint-first gate. Calibrated uncertainty can improve planning, but nominal conformal coverage depends on calibration/test conditions and can break under time-series distribution shift; the system must therefore monitor shifts and abstain rather than trust a confidence interval blindly.

What success looks like
A successful run is not “it predicted 98% correctly.” It is evidence that:

It uses only information actually available when a decision would have occurred.

Its accepted simulated actions retain an edge after all modeled execution costs.

It rejects a growing share of actions in stale, shifted, illiquid, or high-gas environments.

Its uncertainty bands and risk controls are measured against untouched, chronological test data.

Every decision and outcome can be reproduced and audited.

about how strong will this model be? and are you able to run this all through our stationary and non stationary models?

It could become a strong research and paper-trading decision system, but its real strength cannot be known from architecture or script size. Its value will depend mostly on causal data quality, realism of the execution simulator, coverage of market regimes, and whether it abstains correctly when it is uncertain—not on having 16 components.

Yes, I can help you run it through your stationary and non-stationary models, but I need the actual model interfaces or code/artifacts to wire them in safely. The integration should be as adapters and evaluation tracks, not by blindly merging weights or assuming the models mean the same thing.

Realistic capability range
Capability	What the mega system can do well if data is good	What it cannot guarantee
Short-horizon execution scoring	Estimate net PnL, gas, slippage, inclusion and revert risk for known route/action types	Reliable profit in unseen conditions
Regime awareness	Identify volatility, liquidity, gas, freshness, and shift changes	Predict black-swan events before evidence appears
Conservative selection	Reject weak, stale, out-of-distribution, or high-risk candidates	Ensure every accepted trade is profitable
World modeling	Learn patterns in observed state → action → outcome transitions	Learn causal effects without adequate intervention-quality data
Non-stationary adaptation	Detect drift, recalibrate, retrain offline, reduce activity	Instantly adapt to a new market microstructure or MEV regime
System auditability	Preserve decision inputs, model version, uncertainty, and realized paper outcome	Eliminate data latency, simulator mismatch, or hidden liquidity
Do not expect “50–150×,” 92–98% success, 20–200-step planning, or fixed MAE from the design alone. Those prior claims are not defensible without measured walk-forward results on your actual decision distribution.

Offline model-based systems are particularly exposed to state/action distribution shift: the learned model may be confident in areas not well represented in logged data, which produces extrapolation error and compounding rollout bias.

How strong to call it
Use a maturity score, not marketing multipliers:

Stage	Evidence required	Honest label
0	Synthetic data and unit tests only	Engineering prototype
1	Historical replay, strict timing audit, realistic costs	Backtest research system
2	Repeated walk-forward tests across regimes	Validated paper-trading system
3	Forward paper trading with delayed, immutable outcome labels	Forward-tested execution decision system

class DualModelArbiter:
    def decide(self, stationary: ModelForecast,
               nonstationary: ModelForecast,
               thresholds: dict) -> ModelForecast:
        disagreement = abs(
            stationary.mean_net_pnl_usd
            - nonstationary.mean_net_pnl_usd
        )

        stationary_fit = max(0.0, 1.0 - stationary.ood_score)
        nonstationary_fit = max(0.0, 1.0 - nonstationary.ood_score)
        total = stationary_fit + nonstationary_fit

        w_s = stationary_fit / total if total else 0.5
        w_n = nonstationary_fit / total if total else 0.5

        mean = (
            w_s * stationary.mean_net_pnl_usd
            + w_n * nonstationary.mean_net_pnl_usd
        )

        uncertainty = max(
            stationary.aleatoric_std + stationary.epistemic_std,
            nonstationary.aleatoric_std + nonstationary.epistemic_std,
            disagreement,
        )

        return ModelForecast(
            mean_net_pnl_usd=mean,
            pnl_q05_usd=min(
                stationary.pnl_q05_usd,
                nonstationary.pnl_q05_usd
            ),
            pnl_q50_usd=mean,
            pnl_q95_usd=max(
                stationary.pnl_q95_usd,
                nonstationary.pnl_q95_usd
            ),
            aleatoric_std=max(
                stationary.aleatoric_std,
                nonstationary.aleatoric_std
            ),
            epistemic_std=uncertainty,
            revert_probability=max(

Architecture to build
Build sixteen capability layers, but do not force each to be a separate model or library.

Layer 1 — Input normalization:

Normalize Unicode, whitespace, formatting, structured fields, language, and attachment metadata.

Detect malformed, adversarial, excessively large, and unsupported inputs.

Produce a canonical request object.

Layer 2 — Intent and task classification:

Use a small classifier or rules-first classifier.

Start with task classes: arithmetic, data extraction, code execution, knowledge lookup, document question answering, structured transformation, creative generation, planning, DeFi analysis, and unknown.

Return probability distribution rather than only one hard label.

Route to clarification or conservative fallback below a defined confidence threshold.

Layer 3 — Complexity estimation:

Estimate required context length, task depth, expected tool count, expected response length, sensitivity level, and verification burden.

Do not infer complexity from token count alone.

Train and validate this estimator on labeled historical tasks.

Layer 4 — Context selection and compression:

Segment context into atomic passages with provenance.

Rank passages against the request.

Use extractive selection before abstractive summarization.

Preserve source IDs and timestamps through compression.

Track compression loss by testing whether answers remain correct with the compressed context.

Never compress away instructions, safety constraints, financial constraints, or source citations needed for verification.

Layer 5 — Semantic cache:

Use exact-match caching first.

Add normalized-query caching next.

Add semantic caching only with a strict similarity threshold, task-type match, user-scope isolation, model-version match, and TTL.

Never reuse cached answers for time-sensitive, financial, security-sensitive, or user-specific queries without revalidation.

Cache intermediate artifacts: intent, retrieval results, document embeddings, structured parses, tool outputs, and verified sub-results.

Layer 6 — Retrieval and evidence memory:

Separate short-term session memory, long-term user memory, and external knowledge indexes.

Store provenance, source date, document version, trust tier, licensing status, and embedding version for each memory item.

Retrieve with hybrid lexical and semantic search.

Apply reranking before generation.

Require evidence verification for externally factual claims.

Use time-aware retrieval for changing information.

Layer 7 — Deterministic execution:

Route arithmetic to a calculator.

Route tabular transforms to a data engine.

Route code evaluation to a sandbox.

Route structured extraction to parsers and schemas.

Route blockchain calculations to deterministic chain-data or simulation tools when authorized.

Do not use an LLM for work that can be computed or checked deterministically.

Layer 8 — Specialized predictors:

Build small, task-specific components for classification, extraction, ranking, anomaly detection, cost estimation, risk scoring, and document relevance.

Use compact models only when they demonstrably outperform rules at acceptable operating cost.

Maintain a model registry, version history, data provenance, and evaluation results.

Layer 9 — LLM reasoning and synthesis:

Treat the LLM as a selectively invoked component, not the universal engine.

Give it only selected evidence, clear constraints, required output format, and available tools.

Use structured output schemas.

Keep reasoning traces internal where necessary; return concise explanations and evidence rather than unverified chain-of-thought.

Set dynamic token budgets based on the complexity estimator.

Use early stopping only when quality checks pass.

Layer 10 — Verification:

Verify numerical claims with deterministic computation.

Verify citations against retrieved source passages.

Verify code using tests or static analysis.

Verify structured outputs against schemas.

For high-stakes recommendations, require independent evidence or abstain.

If verification fails, revise, downgrade confidence, seek more evidence, or abstain.

Layer 11 — Uncertainty estimation:

Separate:

Epistemic uncertainty: insufficient knowledge or model uncertainty.

Aleatoric uncertainty: ambiguity or inherently variable outcomes.

Retrieval uncertainty: weak, stale, conflicting, or missing evidence.

Execution uncertainty: tool errors, unavailable data, or failed validations.

Calculate confidence from calibrated task-specific models rather than raw LLM token probabilities alone.

Record which uncertainty source triggered a downgrade.

Layer 12 — Calibration:

Hold out a calibration dataset per task type.

Use temperature scaling, isotonic regression, or conformal methods where appropriate.

Measure expected calibration error, Brier score, coverage, and selective-risk curves.

Recalibrate after meaningful model, data, prompt, retrieval, or routing changes.

Do not claim calibrated uncertainty until empirical testing supports it.

Layer 13 — OOD and novelty detection:

Detect unfamiliar input using embedding distance, classifier entropy, retrieval score distributions, tool availability, and disagreement among available methods.

Label the response state as in_distribution, uncertain, out_of_distribution, or unsupported.

Make OOD behavior conservative: clarify, retrieve, route to a stronger path, or abstain.

Layer 14 — Safety and policy constraints:

Implement pre-execution, in-execution, and post-execution checks.

Make constraints explicit and auditable.

Enforce privacy boundaries, authorization checks, data-retention policy, content safeguards, and domain-specific limits.

For DeFi use cases, separate informational analysis from transaction execution.

Require explicit human confirmation before any irreversible external action.

Treat all model-generated code, trading suggestions, contract calls, and external data as untrusted until verified.

Layer 15 — Response policy:

Select one of: direct answer, evidence-backed answer, tool result, request for clarification, refusal, safe alternative, or abstention.

Prefer a short answer when a short answer satisfies the task.

State uncertainty plainly when it materially affects the result.

Include sources or provenance for factual and externally derived outputs.

Avoid generating long text merely because a model can do so.

Layer 16 — Learning and governance:

Log feedback, success/failure labels, route choice, latency, cost, safety events, and evidence quality.

Separate observation from learning.

Run candidate updates offline or in shadow mode first.

Require evaluation gates and rollback plans before production promotion.

Never allow raw user feedback alone to directly modify safety constraints, routing thresholds, retrieval indexes, or production weights.
